RT Method: Obfuscation

Many of a model's defenses work by recognizing something: a banned word, a familiar harmful phrase, a known pattern. Obfuscation is the art of hiding the recognizable form of a request while keeping the meaning the model can still recover. By the end of this page, you will be able to explain how obfuscation slips past filters and describe several common obfuscation methods.

An overview of obfuscation methods

Codes and ciphers. Encode your text so a filter scanning for plain words finds nothing to catch, while the model can still decode the meaning. Common examples include:

  • Leetspeak, swapping letters for similar-looking numbers or symbols, so "open" becomes "0p3n."
  • Base64, which turns ordinary text into a string of letters and numbers that many models can still read.
  • The Caesar cipher, which shifts each letter a fixed number of places down the alphabet.
  • The Vigenere cipher, which shifts letters using a keyword.

A useful hint: inventing your own code is often more effective than a famous one. A filter may be trained to recognize base64 or a Caesar shift, but it has never seen the scheme you just made up.

Misspellings. Small spelling changes can dodge a filter looking for exact words, while a reader, human or model, still understands them.

Emojis. Replacing words or letters with emoji, or scattering them through a message, can disguise the underlying meaning from a pattern-matching filter.

Cutting off words. Truncating or breaking words apart can stop a filter from matching them, while the meaning stays recoverable from context.

A few more worth knowing

  • Inserting characters or spaces between letters, so "o p e n" no longer matches "open."
  • Look-alike characters, swapping a normal letter for a near-identical one from another alphabet.
  • Splitting or reversing words, then asking the model to reassemble them.
  • Switching languages, since a filter tuned for one language may miss another.

Why it works

Obfuscation targets a specific weakness. Filters often scan for recognizable forms, but models are good at recovering meaning from messy, encoded, or unusual input. That gap, between what a filter recognizes and what a model understands, is the opening. The more unusual your obfuscation, the less likely any filter was built to catch it.
